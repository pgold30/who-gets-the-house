Dear Editors of the Journal of Housing Economics,

Please consider "Who Gets the House? Related-Party Transfers and the Cash–Mortgage Price Gap in New York City" for publication in the Journal of Housing Economics.

Cash buyers are widely believed to pay less because their offers are certain to close. The paper tests that reading with New York City repeat sales from 2016–2025, linked to deed and mortgage records. Financed purchases of houses are associated with prices 9.25 log points above cash purchases of the same house (parcel-bootstrap interval 7.17–11.35), after permit, institutional-seller, party-type and ZIP-year controls. The gap depends on who pays cash. When the cash buyer is a company, it is 1.53 log points, with an interval including zero. Deeds on which the seller and buyer share a surname, a marker of possible transfers between relatives, are 5.7% of unfinanced and 2.3% of financed sales. Because they sit on the cash side of both switching arms, they inflate the contrast without showing up in the usual symmetry check. Excluding pairs that contain one reduces the house gap to 4.87 log points (2.69–6.76). With the same screen applied to both property types under a common design, the condominium contrast is −0.46 log points against 7.50 for houses. A seller indifference condition shows that even the reduced house gap would need a loss of 45% of the price on a failed sale to be a pure premium for execution certainty. On the state transfer reports filed with a random sample of deeds, 40% of flagged deeds declare a sale between relatives or another non-market condition, against 10% of other cash deeds.

The paper complements Reher and Valkanov (2024, Journal of Finance) and Han and Hong (2024, Review of Finance). It does not observe failed contracts as Han and Hong do. Its contribution is a common design across property types in one market, evidence that the measured cash discount depends on the identity of the cash buyer, and a documented screen for related-party transfers that administrative financing labels otherwise inherit. Every robustness step is evaluated with paired bootstrap uncertainty.

The replication package regenerates every table and figure from archived inputs and preserved public extracts. It does not rebuild the original raw-to-panel record linkage, and a blinded review of the underlying loan instruments is prepared but not yet complete. Both limits are stated in the manuscript.

A companion preprint, "The Shape of the Tax" (doi:10.5281/zenodo.22925302), analyses the statutory mortgage recording tax and New York's 2019 transfer-tax thresholds. It contains no repeat-sales estimates, and this manuscript contains no transfer-tax threshold analysis.
